WHY EACH ITEM MATTERS — ANALYSIS AND REQUIRED RESOLUTION
1. Density ledger: Obtain signed transfer instruments and the controlling DRI exhibit. Track donor balance, transferred units, recipient balance and prior commitments. Different donor labels may reflect revisions, but an auditor must be able to reproduce the final allocation without double counting.
2. Open space: Require a GIS exhibit and area schedule tying every figure to one plan revision. Separate gross tract area, converted area, qualifying remaining open space and resort-wide compliance. The 3.22-acre difference is about 51% of 6.3 acres; it could reflect different scopes rather than excess development.
3. Land use: Require staff to correct the labels and cite the controlling map, development order and applicable standards. A template error is plausible. The question is whether the actual analysis used the correct rules.
4. Boundaries: Reconcile gross versus net site area, bridge/access land, phased limits and surveyed boundaries. Minor rounding alone is weak evidence. Boundary differences become material if they alter permit eligibility or compliance calculations.
5. Phase II: Require a clear statement of what approval authorizes and what remains conceptual. Obtain separate and combined buildout assumptions. Do not describe all 46 dwellings as approved.
6. Buildout assumptions: Model the maximum enforceable roof, driveway, road and other hardscape allocation. If final individual-home approvals can exceed the modeled assumptions, the hydrologic analysis may not represent buildout.
7. Review closure: Request each reviewer’s dated sign-off and response matrix, tied to the final plans. Distinguish issues cured before approval from conditions legally deferred to construction permitting.
8. Floodplain versus drainage: Passing one review does not settle the other. Ask which analyses address offsite discharge, downstream capacity and backwater.
9. Storage: Obtain stage-storage tables, surveyed elevations, normal water levels, seasonal groundwater, outlet controls and routing results. Storage volume alone cannot establish protection. Confirm that the two reported volumes describe comparable things and that existing storage is not counted twice.
10. Permit route: Obtain the permit number or actual certification and written agency confirmation of applicability. NWFWMD describes the small-project route as involving less than 10 acres total project area and less than two acres impervious surface, subject to conditions. A 9.6-acre figure alone does not establish eligibility. Ask how connected phases, road/access improvements and ultimate hardscape are counted; do not allege project segmentation without evidence. FLOODING — TECHNICAL QUESTIONS FOR AN INDEPENDENT ENGINEER
The following are proposed review tasks, not findings that the design fails.
• Trace the actual drainage network from each project outfall through ponds, pipes, bridges, culverts and receiving waters. Name the final receiving water only after verifying the connection.
• Obtain editable model inputs and outputs, software/version, survey datum, drainage boundaries, soils, infiltration testing, seasonal-high-groundwater data, rainfall inputs and downstream boundary conditions.
• Compare existing and proposed peak flow, runoff volume, maximum water level and inundation duration at every discharge and vulnerable neighboring location. A lower peak can coexist with more volume or longer flooding.
• Check whether a “new” pond occupies existing flood storage and whether groundwater leaves the claimed storage available when needed.
• Include offsite contributing drainage, shared-system capacity and recorded rights to use it. A pipe being present does not prove capacity or authorization.
• Test the applicable regulatory events. Also request transparent resilience sensitivity runs for larger storms, wet antecedent conditions, consecutive storms, high receiving-water levels, restricted outlets, construction-phase runoff and credible equipment failures. Identify which runs are mandatory versus additional requested protection.
• Require an emergency overflow map showing flow paths, low points, neighboring finished floors, road access and safe discharge.
• Compare model behavior with dated resident photos, high-water marks, rainfall records and historical maintenance/complaint records. Record uncertainty and do not treat every historical flood as caused by this proposal.

ESTUARY AND WATER QUALITY — REVIEW AND DESIGN REQUESTS
No verified outfall-to-estuary route or project-specific pollutant export calculation was obtained. Obtain those before making pollution claims.
• Map receiving waters and verify their classifications, impairment listings and applicable basin requirements.
• Require predevelopment and postdevelopment annual nitrogen, phosphorus and suspended-solids estimates using a consistent drainage area, defensible land-use assumptions and documented treatment performance.
• The existing golf practice use may already contribute nutrients. Compare measured or justified existing loads against realistic residential loads; do not assume either land use is cleaner.
• Show treatment during routine storms and the paths taken by overflow or bypass. Show where construction dewatering will discharge and how sediment will be controlled.
• Have the permitting agency identify the applicable stormwater rule edition, any transition/grandfathering basis and project-specific water-quality requirements in writing. Do not assume the DRI’s age determines the applicable standards.
• Consider a treatment train appropriate to site conditions, native buffers, fertilizer limits, irrigation controls and low-impact designs. High groundwater can limit infiltration; require site-specific justification.
• Collect a scientifically designed baseline upstream and downstream of outfalls before disturbance, with rainfall and flow context. Continue paired storm-event monitoring during construction and after occupancy. Parameters, methods, frequency and thresholds should be set by qualified professionals.
• Include quality assurance, laboratory methods and sampling locations. A single turbid sample is not reliable proof of project-caused estuary damage.

PROPOSED CONDITIONS IF THE PROJECT MOVES FORWARD
These are negotiating objectives subject to engineering and legal review, not claims about requirements already imposed.
1. One controlling plan set, density ledger, open-space exhibit and reviewer closure matrix identified in the approval.
2. Independent drainage peer review with written responses to all material comments; a transparent funding arrangement that preserves reviewer independence.

4. Enforceable hardscape limits matching the accepted model. Require review before later additions exceed them.
5. A receiving-water protection plan with treatment performance, erosion/dewatering controls, monitoring points and corrective-action procedures.
6. Construction controls in place and inspected before disturbance; staged clearing, stabilization deadlines and documented response to discharge problems.
7. Surveyed as-builts, field verification and engineer certification before the relevant completion/occupancy milestone.
8. A named stormwater operator, inspection schedule, maintenance access, adequate reserve funding and enforceable repair duties.
9. Defined reporting, exceedance response and enforcement procedures within the county/agency’s authority; specify who may suspend work and under what conditions.
10. Future phases or material design changes require the applicable further review and updated cumulative drainage analysis.
